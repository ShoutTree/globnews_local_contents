Young Britons Choose Medieval Art Over Hollywood Blockbusters

Glob News Desk · AI-assisted, human-reviewed · 2026-10-27 09:24 · 2 min read

Data shows UK youth spending on Bayeux Tapestry tickets outpaces major film releases, signaling a shift in cultural priorities.

A thousand-year-old piece of embroidery has unexpectedly become the center of a modern pop culture phenomenon, surpassing the box office appeal of recent Hollywood blockbusters among young British audiences. Data from payment provider Monzo reveals that visitors under the age of 35 are prioritizing tickets to see the Bayeux Tapestry over cinema outings, marking a surprising moment for medieval art in the digital age.

The surge in interest became evident last Wednesday when the second round of tickets for the exhibition went on sale. In the first hour alone, Monzo cardholders spent £14,100 at the British Museum, a figure that was 48 times higher than the bank’s average spending on a typical Wednesday morning. Notably, 55% of these transactions were made by individuals aged under 35, highlighting a strong demographic shift toward historical engagement.

This financial enthusiasm stands in stark contrast to the opening mornings of this year’s major film releases. According to Monzo’s spending data across its 16 million customers, ticket sales for the critically acclaimed film The Odyssey reached only £7,000, while the blockbuster Spider-Man: Brand New Day generated £6,300. The tapestry’s immediate revenue in just one hour doubled the opening mornings' earnings of these highly anticipated cinematic events.

Despite the high cost of admission, which is significantly steeper than an average movie ticket, the demand remains robust. Standard adult peak tickets are priced at £33, with off-peak options available for £27. When adjusted for these higher entrance fees and the limited availability of seats compared to wide-release films, the per-customer spending power directed toward the tapestry is even more impressive. Buyers spent an average of £97 during checkout, five times the average spend on Spider-Man tickets, which stood at £19.

The appeal of the exhibition extends far beyond London, drawing enthusiasts from across the country. Nearly half of the buyers were based outside the capital, with purchases recorded as far north as Glasgow. This national reach underscores the significance of the event, as the tapestry is currently on display in England for the first time since it was taken to France in the 11th century.

Ticket availability was extremely competitive, with thousands joining an online queue when sales began at 10 am. Spots sold out within three hours, a much faster pace than the first wave of tickets, which took 24 hours to clear. The frenzy has drawn comparisons to major music festivals like Glastonbury and reunion tours, illustrating the intense public desire to witness the woven record of the Norman Conquest.

The British Museum described the response to the exhibition, which opened last month, as extraordinary. To manage the overwhelming crowds and lengthy queues, museum officials implemented new measures, including banning photography inside the gallery. These changes aim to preserve the integrity of the viewing experience while accommodating the massive influx of visitors eager to see the artifact up close.

Critics and curators have praised the exhibition for its meticulous presentation of the textile’s technical prowess and storytelling. The Guardian described the work as 'a stupefying, thrilling, horrifying masterpiece that shakes the earth.' The combination of historical value and artistic skill continues to resonate with modern audiences, proving that ancient narratives can still captivate contemporary sensibilities.
