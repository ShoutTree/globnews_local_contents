Golden Eagle Chick Marks Milestone for Scottish Conservation

Glob News Desk · AI-assisted, human-reviewed · 2026-10-30 08:47 · 2 min read

A golden eagle chick fledging on a managed grouse moor in southern Scotland signals a major breakthrough in species recovery and landowner cooperation.

The arrival of a golden eagle chick, named Beaky Junior, on a managed grouse moor in southern Scotland has been hailed as a significant milestone for the conservation of the UK’s largest bird of prey. The chick successfully fledged from a nest in the Moffat Hills, marking the first time such an event has occurred at a site used for recreational gamebird shooting. This achievement underscores a growing partnership between conservationists and land managers in reversing the decline of golden eagles in the region.

Beaky Junior hatched in a secret location within the Moffat Hills, an area historically associated with gamebird shooting. The success of this nesting is particularly notable given that a recent report by the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds (RSPB) revealed that two-thirds of persecution incidents involving birds of prey in the UK had taken place at similar sites. Despite these challenges, the fledging of Beaky Junior demonstrates that coexistence is possible when stakeholders work together.

Dr. Cat Barlow, chief executive of Restoring Upland Nature (Run), the organization behind the project, described the event as a hugely significant milestone. Run initiated a scheme in the summer of 2018 to relocate golden eagles from the Highlands to the Moffat Hills. At the start of the project, breeding pairs in southern Scotland had dwindled to just a handful. Today, numbers have reached a record level of more than 50, despite facing setbacks including shootings and disappearances.

The birth of Beaky Junior is one of five golden eagle chicks that have successfully fledged in the area this year from nests established by translocated birds. These chicks represent a tangible sign of hope in the battle to reverse biodiversity decline. Dr. Barlow emphasized that the support from landowners and gamekeepers was vital to this success, noting that their involvement ranged from assisting with translocations to monitoring and protecting birds and eyries on their estates.

In recognition of the key role played by local gamekeepers in helping golden eagles thrive, Beaky Junior was named by them. Mark Ewart, regional coordinator at the Southern Uplands Moorlands Group, stated that it is incredibly rewarding to see gamekeepers celebrated for their contributions. He noted that monitoring habitats and ensuring conditions for other species to flourish are part of the daily work of gamekeepers, making the presence of golden eagles a strong indicator of project success.

The RSPB report highlighted 182 confirmed persecution incidents involving birds of prey in Scotland over a ten-year period. While these incidents were scattered across the country, 64.3% occurred on land managed for gamebird shooting. Against this backdrop, efforts in southern Scotland to encourage collaboration between estates and conservationists are viewed as particularly important for the future survival of the species.

Michael Clarke, who chairs Run, remarked that individuals from all walks of life have helped the birds thrive. The organization plans to continue monitoring the impact of golden eagles on the area with the support of land managers, farmers, and the wider community. Sarah-Jane Laing, chief executive of Scottish Land and Estates, expressed delight that gamekeepers named the chick, calling it a powerful example of what can be achieved through trust and genuine partnership.

Laing added that seeing this magnificent species return and flourish in these landscapes is something everyone can take pride in. The success in southern Scotland may pave the way for further expansion of the golden eagle project into England. As the fledglings take to the sky, they offer a glimpse of a future where conservation goals and traditional land management practices align to protect Scotland’s natural heritage.
