Norfolk Couple Wins Landmark Legal Battle to Save Rare Wetland

Glob News Desk · AI-assisted, human-reviewed · 2026-10-25 21:22 · 3 min read

A farming couple in Norfolk has secured three major legal victories against environmental regulators, forcing action to protect Catfield Fen from agricultural pollution.

Tim and Geli Harris, a farming couple in the Norfolk Broads, have achieved a historic legal triumph after more than a decade of campaigning to protect Catfield Fen. In a recent High Court ruling, Lady Justice Lieven found that Natural England failed in its statutory duty to prevent pollution from washing into this internationally important wetland. The decision marks the third landmark victory for the couple, who have spent £1.5 million of their own money holding government agencies accountable for environmental negligence.

Catfield Fen is a rare calcareous fen within the Broads National Park, serving as a habitat for approximately 2,500 species, including the fen orchid, breeding bitterns, and swallowtail butterflies. The court found that nitrates and phosphates from nearby arable fields were contaminating the site. Judge Lieven described the cause of the pollution as requiring only "the most basic level of common sense" to address, criticizing the conservation watchdog’s inaction since the Harrises first reported the issue in 2021.

The legal battles began in earnest when the couple purchased their 460-acre farm in 1994 with the intention of living closer to nature. They converted their arable fields into pasture to reduce soil erosion and pollution runoff. Over the years, they have maintained English white park cattle, sheep, and rare-breed Eriskay ponies on the land. Their efforts to preserve the marshes have involved extensive monitoring and direct engagement with regulatory bodies, despite facing significant opposition from some local farming groups.

This latest judgment follows two previous wins. In 2016, the Harrises won a public inquiry that proved water companies and local farmers were extracting too much groundwater, causing the fen to dry out. In 2022, they successfully forced the Environment Agency to limit water abstraction near other nature-rich sites in the Norfolk Broads. These victories have led to noticeable improvements in water levels and biodiversity in the fen’s dykes, though the couple remains cautious about the long-term health of the ecosystem.

Tim Harris, a former senior figure in the shipping industry, noted that regulatory inertia is a widespread problem affecting waterways across the country. He compared the bureaucratic language used by officials to George Orwell’s "1984," highlighting the disparity in resources between campaigners and polluters. While he respects the individual staff at Natural England, he criticized the organization for lacking the drive necessary to enforce environmental protections effectively.

The couple’s stance has strained relationships within the local farming community. Representatives from the National Farmers’ Union reportedly rejected their application for membership in the local branch, citing discontent among members over restrictions on water abstraction for lucrative crops like potatoes. Tim Harris emphasized that they are not anti-farmer but believe that sustainable practices are essential for the survival of the landscape.

Despite their success, the Harrises express concern over declining insect numbers observed during summer surveys. They argue that arable farmers should be compensated for creating grassland buffer strips between plowed fields and ditches to filter out nitrate-rich soils. Tim Harris also called for regulatory bodies to invest more heavily in monitoring and compliance, fearing that many environmental breaches go unnoticed due to insufficient resources.

As the judge prepares to issue further rulings on specific measures Natural England must take to stop the pollution, the Harrises remain focused on practical solutions. They believe the Broads Authority should take a leading role in combating pollution, but they are considering additional legal challenges if necessary. For now, they view the fen as a "Noah’s ark" where nature can recover and spread outward once conditions improve.

At 79, Tim Harris admits he would prefer to spend his time reading books rather than fighting legal battles. However, he sees the campaign as vital for preserving a landscape that took 1,000 years to create. The couple’s relentless dedication has set a precedent for environmental accountability, proving that private citizens can challenge powerful institutions to protect critical habitats.
