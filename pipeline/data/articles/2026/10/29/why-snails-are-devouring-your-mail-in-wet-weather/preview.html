Why Snails Are Devouring Your Mail in Wet Weather

Glob News Desk · AI-assisted, human-reviewed · 2026-10-29 11:31 · 2 min read

Ecologists explain why garden snails are attracted to letterboxes during humid conditions and offer safe ways to protect your post.

In the age of instant digital communication, the term 'snail mail' has become a nostalgic joke. However, for residents in parts of Australia and the United Kingdom, this phrase is becoming uncomfortably literal. During periods of wet weather, garden snails are increasingly seeking shelter inside letterboxes, where they encounter a surprising feast: paper letters. This phenomenon has led to closed postboxes and frustrated citizens whose correspondence is being consumed by gastropods.

The issue has gained significant attention after ecologist Simon Mustoe revealed that snails had eaten his personal letter from Sir David Attenborough. A BBC report subsequently highlighted the closure of a Royal Mail postbox in Northumberland due to snails munching on the mail. Meanwhile, online forums for Australian hardware chain Bunnings are filled with desperate claims from homeowners who swear snails have a real appetite for postal letters, with some reporting that a single snail can devour an entire letter in just two days.

Comedian Jimeoin recently joked about the situation after moving to Australia, finding a snail eating his mail and remarking, 'You stupid snail, you’re supposed to eat lettuce, not letters.' While humorous, the behavior is rooted in biology. Dr. John Stanisic, known as the Snail Whisperer, explains that letters are processed vegetable matter, making them a logical food source for these molluscs. He noted that snails have even been known to escape containers and eat documents left nearby.

Snails are ancient creatures, with a lineage dating back 350 million years. They play a critical role in ecosystems by consuming waste. Ecologist Simon Mustoe reasons that this function may have helped prevent the late Devonian mass extinction caused by a surge in plant evolution. Dr. Isabel Hyman from the Australian Museum emphasizes their importance in the food web, noting that while people often focus on mammals and birds, a flourishing ecosystem relies on small invertebrates like snails.

Despite their ecological value, the common garden snail, introduced to Australia from Europe 120 years ago, is considered a pest. Unlike native species, which struggle in disturbed habitats, these introduced snails thrive in urban environments. In humid conditions, they seek refuge in letterboxes primarily for moisture. Snails require water to produce mucus, which they use to create slime trails for movement and to keep their bodies hydrated. A dry letterbox offers a damp sanctuary, and the presence of paper provides an unexpected bonus meal.

Controlling these pests without harming local wildlife is a challenge. Dr. Hyman warns against using chemical poisons, which can kill natural predators such as blue tongue lizards, birds, and turtles. Mustoe notes that snail pellets have also been fatal to pets, including French bulldogs. Instead, experts recommend non-toxic barriers. Copper tape creates an uncomfortable electric charge when it contacts snail mucus, while sharp angles, sand, diatomaceous earth, or crushed eggshells make the area around letterboxes difficult to traverse.

For those willing to take more direct action, iron-based baits are considered safer for the environment than traditional chemicals, though they will still kill native snails if used near bushland. Ultimately, ecologists suggest that humans must adapt to the resilience of these ancient animals. The most effective strategy is pragmatic maintenance: regularly cleaning out letterboxes and checking mail frequently to ensure it does not sit long enough to be consumed.

Key takeaways

  1. Garden snails seek shelter in letterboxes during wet weather, where they consume paper letters.
  2. Letters are made of processed vegetable matter, making them a suitable food source for snails.
  3. Introduced garden snails thrive in urban areas and require moisture to survive and move.
  4. Chemical snail pellets can harm native wildlife and pets; copper tape and physical barriers are safer alternatives.
  5. Regularly cleaning letterboxes is recommended to prevent mail from being devoured.
