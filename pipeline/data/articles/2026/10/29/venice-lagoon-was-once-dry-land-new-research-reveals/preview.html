Venice Lagoon Was Once Dry Land, New Research Reveals

Glob News Desk · AI-assisted, human-reviewed · 2026-10-29 11:27 · 2 min read

Scientists reconstruct how rising seas over 10,000 years transformed inland hills and swamps into the floating city of Venice.

The floating city of Venice is renowned for its unique architecture built upon water, but new research reveals that the land beneath it was once far removed from the sea. Scientists have uncovered evidence showing that the area now known as the Venice lagoon was dry ground extending more than 30 miles inland, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of the region's geological history.

A team led by Samuele Ongaro at the University of Southampton has published findings in Quaternary Science Reviews detailing how dramatic changes in sea level over the past 10,000 years transformed the northern Adriatic coastline. By combining seismic data with sedimentary cores collected from coastal basins in northern Italy, Slovenia, and northern Croatia, the researchers were able to model these shifts and reconstruct the ancient landscapes that existed long before modern settlements arose.

The study indicates that the present-day lagoon was not always a body of water but rather part of a vast terrestrial landscape. Furthermore, the area surrounding Trieste, which is now largely submerged or marshy, was previously characterized by hills overlooking swamps and lagoons. This reconstruction provides a vivid picture of an environment that looked nothing like the watery maze familiar to visitors today.

Starting just over 10,000 years ago, sea levels began to rise in fits and starts across the northern Adriatic. In some periods, the water level increased by up to 25 millimeters per year, a rapid change that forced Mesolithic hunter-gatherer populations to adapt quickly. These early inhabitants had to constantly shift their hunting and fishing activities to keep pace with the rapidly changing coastline and disappearing land.

The researchers identified three distinct phases of dramatic environmental change during this period of rising seas. These fluctuations significantly altered the habitable terrain, pushing human communities further toward higher ground or forcing them to develop new strategies for survival in a wetting world. The pace of change was intense enough to disrupt established patterns of life for thousands of years.

Following these turbulent phases, the region entered a period of relative stability over the last 6,000 years. This stabilization allowed for the gradual formation of permanent settlements and the advent of farming in the landscape we recognize today. The slower rate of sea-level rise provided the necessary conditions for agriculture and community building to take root in the remaining dry patches and elevated areas.

This research highlights the dynamic nature of coastlines and the significant impact climate and geological forces have on human habitation. It underscores that the geography of northern Italy has been in a state of flux for millennia, with the boundary between land and sea moving dramatically back and forth. The current configuration of the Venetian lagoon is just one chapter in a much longer story of transformation.

By analyzing sedimentary cores and seismic readings, scientists can peer back into the deep past to understand how ancient environments supported early human societies. The findings offer valuable context for contemporary discussions about sea-level rise, illustrating how natural processes have reshaped inhabited lands in the past and what that might imply for future coastal management.
