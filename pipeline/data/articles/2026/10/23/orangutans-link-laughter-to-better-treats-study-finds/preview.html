Orangutans Link Laughter to Better Treats, Study Finds

Glob News Desk · AI-assisted, human-reviewed · 2026-10-23 20:13 · 2 min read

New research reveals captive orangutans prefer food associated with peer laughter, suggesting emotions drive animal learning.

Captive orangutans are more likely to choose food rewards when they hear the sound of their peers laughing, according to new research that highlights the significant role emotions play in animal learning. The study, published in the journal Biology Letters, suggests that great apes base their food choices on whether others appear to be having a good time, valuing rewards more highly when associated with positive social cues.

Erica Cartmill, a professor of anthropology and cognitive science at Indiana University and the senior author of the work, explained that the research is part of a larger project exploring whether animals feel joy and how those emotions impact cognition. The team aimed to extend human research findings—which show that emotion and rational thought influence each other—to orangutans, testing if emotional states alter how these primates learn associations.

The researchers worked with six orangutans housed at two zoological institutions. Initially, the apes were trained to search through bowls to find hidden food treats. In subsequent trials, the orangutans were presented with two bowls: one containing a tasty morsel buried under a harmless substance like moss or sawdust, and another containing different material but no treat.

Crucially, just before presenting the bowls, the researchers played recordings. These included sounds of orangutans playing and laughing or environmental noises such as wind. The choice of soundtrack depended on which substance was used to cover the food that day, creating an association between the auditory cue and the physical material.

On the fifth day, both bowls contained a treat buried beneath one of the two substances. The orangutans were then asked to pick a bowl. Results from multiple trials revealed that, overall, the apes preferred to select the bowl containing the substance associated with the sound of laughter. Four of the six orangutans showed a clear preference for this option.

Cartmill suggested that the preference could be rooted in the sound of laughter putting the orangutans in a more positive emotional state while they learned to associate the substance with food. This indicates that it is not only the quantity or frequency of a reward that matters during learning, but also the internal state of the animal at the moment of association.

Dr Adriano Lameira, a primatologist and evolutionary psychologist at the University of Warwick who was not involved in the work, described the results as fascinating. He noted that effectively, hearing others clearly having a good time led the orangutans to place their food order by choosing what their peers seemed to enjoy.

The findings suggest that context, emotional states, and what an animal is feeling or thinking about can carry over into how they remember learned information. Cartmill added that these insights could have applications for humans, particularly in educational environments where mood might influence how students value information.

She noted that while students might learn at similar rates regardless of their mood, they may value information more if they learn it when happy. Conversely, anxiety during learning, such as when studying mathematics, could impact subject preference and future academic pursuits, underscoring the deep link between emotion and cognitive development.
